the simplest, stupidest way of explaining it correctly

01 What it is

You know how a big company keeps its computers in a giant building far away, and your games and websites live there? That giant building is called a cloud. Tillandsias makes a tiny one of those, right inside your own computer, so you never have to send your things to a stranger's building.

It works like a doll house. Your real computer is the table. Tillandsias puts a small clean pretend-computer on the table (on Windows and Mac it makes a little Fedora Linux machine for you; on Linux it just uses one your computer already knows how to make, called Podman). Inside that pretend-computer it puts small rooms called apps. Tillandsias tries hard to only ever say "app" to you, never scary words like "container" — that is actually written down as a rule it has to follow.

The rooms are built with no doors to the outside. They are all on one private hallway, and only one room, the doorman, is allowed to go out and fetch things from the internet. Your passwords and keys live in a locked safe room, and the other rooms only ever get a tiny key that works for a little while, for one thing, and then stops working.

Robot helpers that write code for you are only allowed to play inside the doll house, never out on your table. So if a helper knocks something over, it knocks over pretend furniture.

And here is the nicest part: everything in the doll house is meant to be thrown away. If a room gets broken, nobody tries to glue it. You sweep it into the bin and Tillandsias builds a brand-new one that comes out exactly the same every time. The things you actually care about — your real work — live outside, safe, and never get swept.

It is free for anyone to have, use, read, and change.

02 How it was built

Imagine building a huge Lego castle with lots of friends, in different houses, on different days.

The first rule was: write down what you want before you build it. Those written-down promises are called specs, and each promise has to be so clear that two people could not argue about whether it came true. If a promise says "must," somebody has to build a little machine that presses the promise and shows a green light or a red light. No red-light-machine, no promise.

The second rule was the big one, and it has a long name: you are only ever allowed to get less confused, never more. Every single change has to make one fuzzy thing clearer. If you learn something that makes an old promise wrong, you do not quietly erase it — you write down that you got confused, why, and how you will get unconfused again. Getting less confused always, never more, is what the long name means.

They also learned that green lights are not the same as being right. A green light is a clue, not a proof, and every claim has to say how strong its clues are.

Then, because everybody builds at once: nobody is allowed to erase anybody else's writing. Instead each friend writes on their own brand-new sticky note and drops it in the jar. Nobody rubs anything out, nobody has to wait their turn, and when you want to know what is true, you read every note in the jar in order. Because notes only get added and never changed, everybody reading the jar gets the very same answer, no matter what order the notes arrived in. That trick is used everywhere in Tillandsias.

And they did it in lots of tiny quick tries instead of one long careful think, because many small tries land on the right answer much faster.

no jargon, everyday analogies, the questions you actually have

01 What it is

Think about what happens when you rent a kitchen for an afternoon. You bring your own ingredients, you cook, you clean up, and you leave. The kitchen is not yours, you did not have to build it, and nothing you did in it follows you home. Tillandsias is that kitchen, except it appears inside the computer you already own, on demand, and it disappears when you are done.

Big companies rent out computing space "in the cloud" — someone else's machines, in someone else's building, holding your work. Tillandsias gives you the same kind of clean, ready-made workspace, but it builds it on your own laptop or desktop instead. It quietly sets up a small, sealed-off pretend computer inside your real one, puts the tools in it, and hands it to you ready to use. When you close it, that pretend computer is thrown away, not repaired or reused. Next time, you get a brand-new one that is exactly the same. Sameness every time is the whole point — like a photocopier that gives you an identical copy no matter how many times you press the button.

Is your stuff private? Yes, and unusually so. The people who make Tillandsias run no servers that your copy talks to. There is no account, no sign-up, no usage tracking, no crash reports. Your files stay on your machine because there is nowhere else for them to go. The workspace only ever sees the one folder you deliberately opened — it does not go looking through your drive or read your documents. Any password or login you give it is kept in your own computer's built-in secure storage.

Does it cost money? No. It is free software, released under a licence that lets anyone read, use and share it. You are not renting anything, and there is no monthly bill, because the hardware is the one you already paid for.

Do you need the internet? Only for the ordinary reasons: downloading the program, fetching updates and software ingredients, or signing in to a service you chose. The workspaces themselves are walled off from the internet by default — one single carefully watched doorway handles anything that must go out. It can even run its artificial-intelligence helpers entirely on your own machine, so nothing has to leave it at all, unless you personally decide to use an outside provider.

And if you turn it off? Nothing breaks. Everything temporary is meant to vanish; the things you saved deliberately are kept. If some part of it ever gets confused, the fix is to throw that part away and let a fresh one appear — which is exactly how it was designed to behave.

02 How it was built

The people who built Tillandsias hold themselves to one rule, and everything else follows from it: every step must make the project a little less uncertain than it was before, and that reduction has to be provable, not merely felt. They call it "monotonic reduction of uncertainty under verifiable constraints", which is a mouthful for something quite homely — never let the fog get thicker, and never take your own word for it that it got thinner.

In practice this works like a very disciplined recipe collection. First someone writes down, in plain and unambiguous words, what a piece of the system must do — the recipe. Then someone writes a test that would visibly fail if the real thing stopped matching the recipe — the taste check. Rules that cannot be tested are not allowed, because a promise nobody can check is just a hope. The written recipe, not the machinery, is treated as the truth; if the machinery disagrees, the machinery is wrong.

They are honest about how far this proves anything. Their own notes say plainly that evidence is not proof, and that passing tests can never guarantee perfection — only that a named list of obligations has been met. The recipes themselves keep improving, so the target keeps moving; what they claim is that the gap keeps shrinking release after release, not that it ever reaches zero.

The work is done by many small workers rather than one large one — many quick, cheap attempts that converge on a good answer faster than a single long, careful one would. It is closer to a kitchen of line cooks than a single chef.

Because those workers run on different machines, in different sessions, and sometimes just stop mid-task, the shared to-do list is built on a trick borrowed from distributed systems. Nobody erases or overwrites anyone else's entry. Everyone only ever adds a note to the end of the list, each task carries a name derived from the task itself so two people describing the same job produce the same name, and the true state of things is worked out by reading all the notes together. That way two people writing at once produce two notes rather than a collision, and no coordinator or supervisor is needed. This same merge-don't-clobber idea shows up at every layer of the system.

Finally, everything is dated and traceable. Version numbers are built from the calendar so they always move forward, each released copy points back to the exact recipes and evidence behind it, and every rule in the method has to name where it came from and what would prove it wrong. A rule without that paperwork is treated as an assumption, not a fact.

you've run Docker and self-hosted things; here are the real parts

01 What it is

Tillandsias is a small, private cloud region that lives on your machine and disappears when you stop it. You install one binary; it brings up a Fedora guest, and inside it a rootless Podman stack: tillandsias-vault, a bare git mirror service, tillandsias-proxy (Squid, forward), tillandsias-router (Caddy, reverse), and a per-project forge container that your coding agents run in. They share an internal Podman network created with podman network create tillandsias-enclave --internal, so nothing on it can reach the internet directly. Only the proxy is dual-homed onto an egress network. That's the whole trick: an isolated region with exactly one door.

Getting one running is a curl and a click. On Linux, curl -fsSL .../install.sh | bash, and Podman is the only host dependency. On Windows you run tillandsias-tray.exe and it provisions a Fedora WSL2 distro; on Apple Silicon install-macos.sh provisions a Fedora VM under Virtualization.framework. Then you point it at a project directory and attach. From a terminal that's tillandsias --headless /path/to/project plus --claude, --codex, --opencode, or --bash; from the tray it's the same podman run with a host terminal wrapped around it. --init builds and starts the stack, --reset-guest throws it all away and reprovisions, and --publish-local exposes a project web container.

The rule that shapes everything is forge-as-only-runtime: claude, codex, opencode, and bash exist only inside the forge image, never on your host $PATH, and the tray refuses to launch an agent if the image is missing rather than falling back to a host binary. Mounts into the forge are exhaustively enumerated — your project directory at /home/forge/src/<project>, a read-only enclave CA cert dir, tmpfs, and a per-launch mktemp -d. Your $HOME, ~/.config, ~/.cache, and host keychain are unreachable from inside — there is even a regression test named launch_forge_agent_does_not_mount_user_home.

Ephemeral is the load-bearing word. Uncommitted work inside a forge is gone when the container stops — AGENTS.md puts it in capitals: a finding you did not push is a finding you destroyed. In the default mode the forge doesn't work on your directory at all — it does git clone git://git-service/<project> from the enclave git daemon, and remote.origin.pushurl is rewritten so every agent push transits the mirror's pre-receive relay on its way upstream. (A host-mounted mode exists via TILLANDSIAS_PROJECT_HOST_MOUNT=1.) Idempotent is the companion: every layer is built to come up clean from scratch, so the declared response to a broken container, guest, or stack is destroy-and-recreate, never hand-patch — error messages are required to say so, with the exact command.

What survives stopping the stack (and what does not):

  • The bare mirror in the volume tillandsias-mirror-<project>, plus your host working copy, which the tray fast-forwards from the mirror on every push — non-destructively, skipping any dirty tree, detached HEAD, or non-fast-forward.
  • Vault secrets in the tillandsias-vault-data volume, unsealed from a Shamir share held in your host's native keychain, never a passphrase prompt.
  • The Ollama model cache, which is a host bind mount at ~/.cache/tillandsias/models rather than a volume — so even podman system reset leaves it alone.
  • Not --reset-guest, though: it deletes every tillandsias--prefixed container, volume, and secret — mirrors and vault included — and rebuilds from scratch. Push first. scripts/uninstall.sh is gentler, keeping the VM image and cache unless you pass --wipe.

Everything else — the forge container, the router, the proxy, the network itself — is rebuilt on the next attach. Web services never publish ports: tillandsias-router binds 127.0.0.1:<port> only (80, falling back to 8080) and routes <service>.<project>.localhost to internal ports from a generated dynamic.Caddyfile — opencode to 4096, vite to 5173, next to 3000, jupyter to 8888. Nothing listens on 0.0.0.0.

02 How it was built

The method has a name and it is the project's core principle: monotonic reduction of uncertainty under verifiable constraints. Read it as a loop, not a slogan. Specs declare intent; litmus tests turn each requirement into an observable signal; code implements; traces prove the code ran; CI refuses anything that breaks the chain. Every edit is supposed to reduce uncertainty about one named boundary, and the convergence engine in methodology/convergence.yaml states the objective as minimizing four gaps at once: spec↔code, code↔cheatsheet, cheatsheet↔reality, and litmus-binding↔spec.

Specs live in openspec/specs/ — 177 of them, one directory per capability (forge-as-only-runtime, enclave-network, git-mirror-service, tillandsias-vault, subdomain-routing-via-reverse-proxy), each written as MUST/SHOULD requirements with numbered #### Scenario: blocks. Specs carry a verification level: S0 draft, S1 syntactically valid with an ambiguity score of zero, S2 litmus-bound (every MUST has at least one executable signal), S3 runtime-validated with traces emitting spec linkage. Code annotations carry a parallel L0–L3 ladder, and CI validates the declared level against the observed one — claiming L3 without a passing litmus is itself a failure. Litmus tests are 411 YAML files under openspec/litmus-tests/, wired to specs by openspec/litmus-bindings.yaml, which records a coverage ratio per spec and tombstones the obsolete ones. scripts/ holds 437 shell scripts including 96 check-*.sh guards and 181 test-*.sh — and ./build.sh --check before every push is the only trunk protection, because push CI was deliberately deleted to stop burning the Actions budget — .github/workflows/ holds exactly one file, release.yml, and it is workflow_dispatch only.

Progress is measured, not asserted. CentiColons (methodology/proximity.yaml) are a bounded ranking function over a finite obligation lattice: each spec gets a denominator from weighted obligations — 100 for a MUST, 120 for an invariant, 100 for a negative litmus signal — and obligations earn credit only when the evidence is queryable and the linked artifact exists at the cited revision. "2778/2800 cc" means 22 CentiColons of named, inspectable work remain. The loop then picks the worst residual to attack next rather than the easiest passing test — and it measures closure, not confidence: the methodology is explicit that evidence is not proof, that CentiColons are not probabilities, and that it is not claiming a metric contraction.

The coordination layer is where the CRDT mechanics show. Work is filed as packets in a plan ledger. plan/index.yaml is a compacted base and a declared concurrency block — nobody edits it in place. Instead each host writes new immutable fragments to plan/index.d/, named <utc>-<suffix>-<host>.yaml. Reading the ledger is base ⊕ fold(fragments) in filename order, which is chronological because the name is UTC-first. Two properties make it conflict-free: one writer per file (a host writes a path only it could have produced, so git never conflicts) and immutability after writing (so the fold is order-independent and idempotent). Per field: packets are a G-set keyed by packet_id, events are a G-set, status is an LWW-register resolved by (ts, host) — deliberately not applying LWW to lists, because that would discard the loser's entries. Compaction is text-level and never re-serializes YAML, after a serde_yaml round-trip was found to drop the ledger's inline operator rulings.

  • Versions are epoch-anchored CalVer — <years_since_epoch>.<month>.<day>.<build>, so 56.9.2.1 is 2026-09-02 — from a single VERSION file, never the wall clock, so rebuilding an old tag reproduces its version.
  • Releases ship as prereleases; scripts/promote-stable.sh <tag> flips one to stable and requires curl-install e2e PASS evidence in plan/ naming that exact tag.
  • Convergence is asymptotic across versions, not terminal within one: the enforceable rule is that measured debt must be non-increasing at each release boundary, and the project declines to claim it reaches zero.

trust boundaries, blast radius, provenance — answered before you ask

01 What it is

Tillandsias is a locally-hosted, ephemeral compute enclave for running coding agents against your source. The pitch is containment: you give an LLM agent a shell, and everything it can touch is deliberately bounded. Free software, no telemetry, no vendor backend — PRIVACY.md states there is no server operated by the project that your install talks to, and there is no analytics or crash reporting SDK to contradict it.

Layering. On Linux there is no VM: tillandsias-headless runs on the host and drives rootless Podman directly — README states Podman is the only host dependency there, and crates/tillandsias-vm-layer carries per-OS transports (wsl.rs, vz.rs, transport_windows.rs) with its Linux fake module gated behind a test feature. On Windows the tray provisions a WSL2 Fedora distro; on macOS a Virtualization.framework Fedora guest (no krunkit/libkrun — not evidenced). So the hypervisor boundary you may be assuming exists on Linux does not. Host↔guest control is vsock/HvSocket.

Isolation envelope. crates/tillandsias-podman/src/policy.rs defines MANDATORY_HARDENING_FLAGS — --userns=keep-id, --cap-drop=ALL, --security-opt=no-new-privileges, --security-opt=label=disable — and validate_launch_argv() rejects --privileged, a non-keep-id userns, --cap-add=ALL. The only allowlisted passthrough is --device=<path> (GPU). Containers run as uid 1000. Note the fourth flag honestly: SELinux labelling is disabled on every container; MCS labelling for the forge domain is planned in openspec/specs/default-image/spec.md, not shipped. Seccomp customization: not evidenced.

Network. podman network create tillandsias-enclave --internal — no default route, aardvark-dns resolves container names only. Only the proxy is dual-homed (ENCLAVE_EGRESS_NETS). Squid on :3128 is default-deny against images/proxy/allowlist.txt (207 lines), denials issued as TCP_RESET. There is no iptables/nftables anywhere — enforcement is network placement plus HTTP_PROXY env injection.

The TLS interception question, answered precisely. Yes there is ssl-bump and a trusted CA, but interception is exact-host and narrow: ssl_bump peek at step 1, bump only for SNI release-assets.githubusercontent.com, splice all otherwise. GitHub APIs, package registries, and every auth endpoint stay end-to-end. Upstream verification is retained — tls_outgoing_options cafile=/etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt, with an in-config standing order never to add DONT_VERIFY_PEER. The CA is a self-signed RSA-2048, 30-day cert generated by ensure_ca_bundle into /tmp/tillandsias-ca (openssl req -nodes, rsa:2048) — per-install, not per-launch, and openspec/specs/proxy-container/spec.md's claim of an EC P-256 per-launch root+intermediate chain on tmpfs does not match the code. The private key reaches Squid only as a Podman secret at mode=0400; the entrypoint exit 1s if it is absent. It got there the hard way: 755-qcxh found that key 0644 in /tmp — any local uid could mint certs the enclave trusts — and 772-shi9 found macOS preambles re-widening it. The same CA also signs the nix binary-cache leaf, so it is not solely a MITM key.

Secrets. HashiCorp Vault in a container, enclave-only, file storage on tillandsias-vault-data, TLS leaf from the enclave CA, no host port (except a loopback compat mapping on native Linux). Forge containers get no broad Vault token and, by default, no host project bind mount at all — the workspace is a git clone from a per-project enclave mirror. The GitHub OAuth token never enters the forge: the tillandsias-git-<project> mirror holds AppRole material (/run/secrets/vault-approle, 48h SecretID, client tokens re-minted at their 24h max TTL, sunk to tmpfs only) and relays pushes upstream via a credential helper on stdin, never argv — a fix for a real /proc/<pid>/cmdline leak.

Blast radius, stated bluntly by the repo. podman-idiomatic-patterns/spec.md says a container escape lands as the invoking user's UID on the host, with $HOME. docs/cheatsheets/enclave-architecture.md adds: host compromise is total compromise, and the allowlist is generous by design — DNS- or header-encoded exfil to an allowed domain is possible. The allowlist admits .amazonaws.com, .googleapis.com, .cloudflare.com, .netlify.app — anyone-can-publish namespaces. Since only one host is bumped, there is no payload inspection: the proxy sees SNI/CONNECT hostnames.

Known bypasses, all self-documented. Image builds egress directly with --dns 8.8.8.8 ("outside the trust boundary"). run_provider_login is dual-homed and, measured on two hosts, reaches pypi.org and example.com — "the attachment does not grant 'GitHub push', it grants the internet." The project browser sets no --network at all. Env vars are trivially unset by an agent; only the --internal netns actually stops it. The git:// receive-pack mirror listener is anonymous: "network placement SHALL NOT be described as client authentication."

02 How it was built

The governing method is Monotonic Reduction of Uncertainty under Verifiable Constraints (methodology/philosophy.yaml). Two invariants matter to a security reader: verification_claims_must_be_falsifiable, and evidence_is_not_proof. Green is a signal, not a guarantee, and the repo says so.

Spec-to-code binding. Every active spec must carry a @trace spec:<name> annotation, at least one executable litmus test per MUST, and cheatsheet provenance; ambiguity.score must be 0. scripts/validate-traces.sh refuses ghost traces and orphan specs. Verification is laddered: S0–S3 for spec maturity (S2 = every MUST litmus-bound; S3 = gates PRs and emits runtime trace linkage) and L0–L3 for code annotations, where a declared level is a claim CI re-derives and downgrades on mismatch. The reason security-privacy-isolation reads as a real contract rather than a slogan is that its four "zero-tolerance" boundaries each terminate in a named litmus.

Supply chain — what is genuinely verifiable. Release artifacts are signed with Sigstore cosign keyless: no long-lived key, identity is the GitHub Actions OIDC token, inclusion proof in Rekor, one .cosign.bundle per asset plus signed SHA256SUMS*. scripts/check-release-asset-integrity.sh enforces transitive coverage. Workflow actions are SHA-pinned (one exception: cache-nix-action@v7, tag-pinned). The Linux musl lane is nix/crane with flake.lock rev-pinned nixpkgs.

What is not verifiable, said plainly. The verification identity regexp is https://github.com/.*/tillandsias/ — it does not pin the org, workflow, or ref. There is no SBOM, no cosign attest, no SLSA/in-toto predicate, no container-image signing, and no reproducible-build claim. Container base images are pinned by tag, never by digest (fedora-minimal:44, alpine:3.22, hashicorp/vault:1.18, caddy:2-alpine) — mutable-tag provenance. macOS builds are ad-hoc signed and unnotarized, and the installer clears com.apple.quarantine. Windows .exe/.zip ship unsigned when no Trusted Signing account is configured; unsigned MSIX is now withheld on the honest reasoning that an absent asset beats an inert one. And the advertised curl … | bash one-liner verifies nothing — install.sh is signed, but the pipe does not check it.

Ephemerality as a control, and its limits. Forges run --rm; /tmp, /run/user/1000, /opt/cheatsheets are tmpfs; the browser profile dies with the window; uncommitted in-forge work is destroyed (hence AGENTS.md's "a finding you did not push is a finding you destroyed"). What persists: the per-project git mirror volume (the only durable copy of committed work), the tool cache volume, vault-data and its audit device on a host dir, the shared read-only nix store, and — critically — the Vault Shamir share in the host OS keychain. Ephemerality therefore resets compute, not identity. 900-z3kv proved this: on Linux, podman system reset --force is not credential-cold — three hosts asserted an empty store, then a "pristine" init logged recovered Shamir unseal share from host keychain and preserving existing data volume, meaning the vault re-init path the smoke claims to exercise has never run on Linux. Windows had the mirror-image failure (803-49re): a stale share bricked GitHub login, and the packet was deliberately left open because Part A only helps hosts that wipe after the fix.

Agent autonomy and auditability. Agents execute only inside forges (forge-as-only-runtime); no host-side agent binary is permitted. Two channels deliberately cross back out and are the surfaces to review first: the tray control socket (0600, postcard framing, 64 KiB cap) bind-mounted RW into every forge, and host-browser-mcp, which lets a contained agent drive a host Chromium via browser.eval/click/type. The host↔guest exec path is authenticated (Noise_NNpsk0_25519_ChaChaPoly_BLAKE2s, PSK = HKDF-SHA256 over the running binary's own SHA-256, separated per hop and per build version) and allowlisted (exec_allowlist.rs) — but note what that authenticates: possession of the same released binary, not the identity of the tray. release_root_secret() falls back to a hardcoded DEV_ROOT_SEED under debug_assertions, and its own docstring defers per-boot mixing of a host-controlled secret to an open packet (order 142) — but read plan/issues/security-audit-zero-trust-2026-07-01.md for how it started: VMADDR_CID_ANY, a self-reported Hello, and verbatim argv exec. The audit trail — plan/index.yaml, plan/issues/, plan/mo-full-attestations.d/ — is committed and unusually candid, but it is agent-self-reported and unsigned; no commit signing is evidenced. 940-f77j (a red tree stamped green) is fixed to be "unforgeable by accident, not by intent." Take that as the honest summary of the whole audit posture.

and I'd like you to be condescending about it

01 What it is

Tillandsias is a tray binary that folds a disposable cloud region through your hypervisor. Strip the marketing and the architecture is a four-tier containment funnel — host tray → guest VM → podman enclave → forge container — with exactly one declared execution surface and an argv-level policy checker standing in front of it.

The funnel. The host runs one Tillandsias-owned process: a GTK/SNI tray on Linux (crates/tillandsias-headless, whose main.rs is a 25k-line orchestrator), a Win32 NotifyIcon tray, or an AppKit NSStatusItem — the latter two sharing crates/tillandsias-host-shell, so their platform-specific code is menu plumbing and nothing else. On macOS and Windows that process first materialises a Fedora guest (Virtualization.framework VM, or a WSL2 distro) through crates/tillandsias-vm-layer, whose VmRuntime trait is deliberately the twin of the ContainerRuntime surface in crates/tillandsias-podman. The guest is not a bespoke rootfs: images/vm/manifest.toml pins registry.fedoraproject.org/fedora:44 by digest, per architecture, names Fedora's own artifact URLs and an expected_rootfs_sha, and exposes its recipe_sha as vm.recipe@<sha> in the Hello.capabilities handshake so a stale recipe is detectable rather than merely wrong. Inside the guest, rootless Podman brings up a stack on an --internal network tillandsias-enclave assembled from images/{git,proxy,web,vault,router,inference,chromium,builder, default}. Agents run in the deepest container — the forge — and nowhere else.

That last clause is not aspiration. openspec/specs/forge-as-only-runtime/spec.md forbids Command::new("opencode") and its siblings anywhere in crates/tillandsias-headless/src/ or crates/tillandsias-podman/src/, requires the tray to refuse rather than fall back when the forge image is missing, and declares the mount categories exhaustive: exactly four legal sources — the canonical project workspace at /home/forge/src/<project>, the ephemeral CA-cert dir from ensure_enclave_for_project mounted read-only, --tmpfs, and a per-launch mktemp -d. $HOME, ~/.config, ~/.cache are unreachable by construction, pinned by the regression test the spec names in its own prose, launch_forge_agent_does_not_mount_user_home. An exhaustive-categories requirement is a closed predicate over one argv vector. That is the right shape: falsifiable by inspecting a Vec<String>, not by arguing about least privilege.

The hardening envelope is a pure function. crates/tillandsias-podman/src/policy.rs declares MANDATORY_HARDENING_FLAGS: [&str; 4] = ["--userns=keep-id", "--cap-drop=ALL", "--security-opt=no-new-privileges", "--security-opt=label=disable"] and returns typed LaunchArgvError::{MissingRunSubcommand, MissingImage, MissingMandatoryHardening, WeakensMandatoryHardening}. Note the fourth variant: it catches argv that re-weakens a flag already present (no-new-privileges=false), which is the failure a naïve contains() check misses. The module "deliberately does not execute Podman" — it is an auditable checker, so the property is decidable before any OS process exists. --rm is excluded on purpose; container ephemerality is a per-profile choice, the four flags are not. And the consequence is real: images/router/ strips cap_net_bind_service from /usr/bin/caddy, because the kernel refuses to exec a file-capability binary under --cap-drop=ALL + --security-opt=no-new-privileges. The envelope binds hard enough to break things, which is how you know it is on.

Ephemerality is a stated invariant, not an emergent property. methodology/philosophy.yaml lists runtime_substrate_is_ephemeral_recreate_ never_repair among global_invariants, with a recreate ladder (podman/stack → guest → host tray → release) and an error_message_policy that pushes the word "recreate" into user-facing text. Repair is not a degraded mode here; it is a category error. Idempotence follows: if the substrate is always recreated from a same-revision image, re-running the launch is the identity on observable state. Where idempotence is claimed it is tested — ensure_containers_conf_dns_ servers carries an explicit idempotency test; populate_hot_paths() must be idempotent by spec; cache-isolation states outright that cache deletion is never data loss because "the next launch MUST rebuild missing cache artifacts." Forge staleness is keyed on .last-build-forge.sha256 over source hash only, so a VERSION bump refreshes human aliases and rebuilds nothing; containerfile-staleness forbids comparing file mtimes and requires the runtime image source digest. The four HOT tmpfs roots (/opt/cheatsheets 8MB, /home/forge/src computed, /tmp 256MB/01777, /run/user/1000 64MB/0700) are capped by --tmpfs=<path>:size=<N>m,mode=<oct> — kernel ENOSPC, not advisory — and the spec says of a fifth: "'Maybe a hot path' is a HARD NO."

The transport boundary is cryptographic, and version-bound by derivation rather than comparison. crates/tillandsias-secure-channel secures both hops of the transparent exec chain (host tray ⇄ guest headless over vsock, guest ⇄ container) with PSK = HKDF-SHA256(ikm=release_root_secret, salt="tillandsias-control-channel", info="v=<build_version>;wire=<wire_version>;hop=<hop_id>"). Mismatched releases do not reject each other; they cannot derive the same key, which is strictly stronger than a version check an attacker can lie about — and it is why methodology/convergence.yaml can list "dual plaintext+secure acceptance in the same boot" as a forbidden shortcut. crates/tillandsias-control-wire carries [4-byte BE length][postcard ControlEnvelope] with a #[non_exhaustive] message enum whose variants are tombstoned, never reordered. crates/tillandsias-headless/src/exec_allowlist.rs is a pure function (kept out of pty_handler precisely because that module sits behind a feature combination nobody lints): the verbatim-argv arm requires argv[0] to start with /usr/local/bin/, /usr/bin/ or /bin/, contain no /.., and have a basename that is not any of bash|sh|dash|zsh|ksh|fish|env — after which arguments reach execve untouched, so a$(id)b arrives as one data element. It is advertised as capability ExecArgvVector in HelloAck.server_caps because hosts must feature-detect, never version-compare. Secrets live in an in-enclave Vault (https://vault:8200, AppRole tokens on 1h TTLs, no host port map in VM launches) and the GitHub token never crosses into a forge at all — pushes go through a git push --atomic pre-receive relay in images/git/.

And the receipts, including the unflattering ones. Push CI was removed 2026-08-03; the whole gate is ./build.sh --ci-full on your own metal. What stops an unstamped push is scripts/gate-stamp.sh, the best-engineered thing here: a hook that runs the gate gets --no-verify'd on its second use, so the gate instead stamps a digest over HEAD plus the full working-tree diff including untracked files, and the hook verifies currency for the cost of one hash. Since order 765-dt8h the stamp carries a scope, because --check, --ci and --ci-full validate materially different things and previously wrote byte-identical stamps; the class vocabulary is closed and total, with unrecognised paths mapping to other, which only scope full covers. v1 stamps are refused loudly rather than having a scope inferred — a correct refinement of a partial function into a total one. Against that, the README's own release table records 755-qcxh: "the enclave's TLS-interception CA private key is 0644 in /tmp — any local uid can mint certificates the enclave trusts; squid only reads it because everyone can." The Vault leaf key goes through a podman secret. The MITM CA key did not. Both facts are in the repository, which is more than most projects manage; only one of them is in the marketing.

02 How it was built

The thesis is one sentence, and it is the title of a file: monotonic reduction of uncertainty under verifiable constraints. The eponymous root YAML is now a tombstone pointing at the nine-component split under methodology/; the live statement is methodology/philosophy.yaml's core_principle.

The formalisation, and its refreshingly narrow claims

methodology/math-foundations.yaml is where you should aim your scepticism, and where you will be partly disarmed. The model:

  • An obligation is a named auditable requirement with a stable ID. Its state ranges over the finite chain absent < declared < traced < positively_tested < negatively_tested < runtime_observed < evidence_bundled.
  • spec_state is the product lattice of its obligations' states; project_state the product over active specs, ordered componentwise after aligning stable IDs and tombstones.
  • centicolon_function maps spec_state → ℕ, bounded, "monotone only for evidence transitions that preserve obligation IDs and do not introduce penalties, ambiguity, or denominator scope changes."

Five claims are catalogued with backing refs and — this is the part that matters — explicit limits. math.order.lattice-model@v1 (Tarski 1955, Davey & Priestley) proves monotonicity of the model, not truth of the modelled requirement. math.fixpoint.convergence-target@v1 (Kleene) defines closure as refine(refine(state)) == refine(state) — idempotence of the refinement operator under known validators, explicitly not completeness against unknown requirements. math.abstract-interpretation.evidence-abstraction@v1 (Cousot & Cousot) concedes flatly that no Galois connection is defined, so this is "an abstraction discipline, not a formal abstract interpreter." math.metric.contraction-not-claimed@v1 cites Banach 1922 in order to disclaim it, and specifies what a future claim would owe: a metric d, an operator F, a constant 0 ≤ c < 1 with d(F(x),F(y)) ≤ c·d(x,y). And math.evidence.uncertainty-not-probability@v1 (Shafer, Walley) forbids reading CentiColons as probabilities.

So: no, you cannot dismiss this as lattice cosplay. It is a finite product order, a bounded ranking function in the Floyd/Lyapunov sense, and a fixed point under declared validators, with contraction and Galois connections named as absent. methodology/philosophy.yaml:multi_version_convergence closes the loop: with d_v = d(I_v, T_v) and T a moving target, release-over-release non-increase of d_v gives convergence to a floor d_* ≥ 0 and explicitly not d_* = 0 — that would need a progress premise excluding positive residual fixed points. bar_raise_governance supplies the decidability: the bar is fixed within a release and steps only at operator-cut release boundaries, so each intra-release convergence point is well defined. Discrete operator-gated steps, not a continuously rising goalpost. The reasoning is correct.

Which CRDT discipline appears where — precisely

The word "CRDT" appears in three distinct places with three different degrees of earning it, and the repo distinguishes them itself.

  1. The plan ledger — a real, tested CRDT. crates/tillandsias-plan/src/fragments.rs (4799 lines) implements base ⊕ fold(fragments) over plan/index.yaml plus immutable plan/index.d/<utc>-<suffix>-<host>.yaml. Per-field discipline: packets: is a G-Set keyed by packet_id (union: commutative, associative, idempotent); events: is a G-Set keyed by (packet_id, event identity); fields: (alias status:, order 642-fedr) is an LWW-Register keyed on {packet_id}\u{1}{field}, resolved by (ts, host). Deletion is by tombstone, because a G-Set has no remove and a naïve delete is re-added by any replica that missed it. Determinism comes from folding in (ts, filename) order with UTC-first names — never directory order, "the filesystem promises none". Convergence is pinned by the_fold_is_commutative_the_defining_crdt_property, the_fold_is_idempotent_so_a_half_finished_compaction_is_safe, the_fold_is_order_independent, and rank_aware_merge_is_commutative_end_to_ end among 78 tests in that file. Compaction deletes exactly the fragments it folded by name, never a glob — the GC-versus-writer race, named as such. Note the wrinkle you would have found: status_entry_wins is not a plain LWW-Register. It composes a monotone rank join over closure_rank: implemented(0) < completed(1) < verified(2) < done(3) with LWW as tiebreak at equal rank, treats obsoleted/failed as lateral (plain LWW), and permits descent only on an explicit incoming_falsified flag. That is a lexicographic join with a deliberate non-monotone escape hatch — the same bounded-uncertainty exception the spec system has, expressed in code.
  2. Version metadata — a genuine 2-component join-semilattice. methodology/versioning.yaml argues correctly that SemVer has no natural total order (patch resets, LUB(1.0.1,1.0.1) loses causality) and that a bare counter collides across nodes; a calendar anchor plus a build counter joins componentwise by max, commutatively, associatively, idempotently. scripts/verify-version-monotonic.sh enforces VERSION ≥ latest tag.
  3. **Specs and cheatsheets — deliberately not claimed. methodology/spec-system.yaml:crdt_properties is typed semantic_merge_with_crdt_preconditions, and methodology/cheatsheets.yaml:crdt_model lists preconditions including property_tests_for_commutativity_associativity_idempotence, with the anti-pattern spelled out: calling a lossy semantic cache a CRDT "creates false convergence claims". methodology/provenance.yaml:methodology.crdt. preconditions@v1 files this as claim_strength: external_analogy and keeps the weaker label "CRDT-like" until those property tests exist. They do not exist** — no proptest or quickcheck dependency appears anywhere in the workspace; the fragment tests are example-based. The repo is honest about this. It is still an open obligation, and I am obliged to say so.

The best evidence that the honesty is load-bearing rather than decorative sits in a Lua file. crates/tillandsias-plan/lua/collect.lua opens: "This is a SEEN-SET DEDUP, not a CRDT — the earlier header's CRDT claim (commutativity in particular) was false: first-wins keeps whichever duplicate arrives first, so order matters." A retracted convergence claim, with the property that failed named. Most repositories would have kept the word.

The LUA layers

crates/tillandsias-plan/src/lua_runtime.rs embeds mlua and hot-reloads four scripts (302 lines total: tier.lua, decompose.lua, collect.lua, init.lua, the last loaded last so it may reference the others). The division of labour after order 920-pxg6 is strict: Lua owns tier classification, variant trimming, and collection dedup — deterministic data-in/data-out. Rust owns everything with consequences: dispatch, endpoints, retrieval, envelope construction, and citation validation (answer::verify in pipeline::run_grounded). The dead validate.lua was deleted rather than demoted. The module doc-comment is titled "SECURITY SURFACE, exactly as implemented — a PARTIAL stdlib restriction, not a sandbox", enumerates what new() actually nils (os.execute/exit/getenv, io.open/popen/close/output/ input, debug, loadfile, dofile, require), states that os.remove and os.rename remain reachable, and instructs the reader to treat lua/ as trusted code. It then adds: "Do not re-promise a stronger sandbox here without implementing one — that phantom claim is what the 920-pxg6 audit removed." LatencyTier budgets (Immediate 500ms, Quick 3s, Fine 12s, NonUsable >15s) tie this to philosophy.yaml:convergence_via_velocity, whose argument is that a single prompt is one finite sample converging with skew (weak LLN), so you bound per-prompt skew and let iteration supply strong-LLN almost-sure convergence. Whether that maps onto LLM sampling is an analogy; it is at least the correctly stated analogy, with the hazard named (unbounded terminal skew defeats infinite iteration).

Specs, litmus, and the centicolon wiring

Specs live at openspec/specs/<capability>/spec.md with modality (MUST/MUST_NOT/SHOULD/SHOULD_NOT/MAY), ambiguity.score with allowed: 0, and mandatory tri-binding: @trace, litmus tests, cheatsheets. Verification is two orthogonal ladders — S0–S3 for the spec (draft → syntactically valid → litmus-bound → runtime-validated) and L0–L3 for a code annotation's evidence (spec only → +cheatsheet → +API docs → +passing litmus), under declared_level_is_claim / ci_validates_against_observed_level. Behavioural ambiguity is defined operationally: run behaviourally distinct candidates against the same litmus; if contract-relevant outcomes differ while all pass, the spec is ambiguous and activation is blocked.

Litmus tests are 411 YAML files in openspec/litmus-tests/ (34,606 lines), each with spec:, phase: (fast_check/pre-build/post-build/runtime), a size: tier with wall-clock budgets, and a critical_path: of shell steps. Read litmus-added-fragment-parse-gate-shape.yaml before you dismiss the genre: it carries negative controls in hermetic throwaway git repos — a bare ts: 2026-08-12T15:31:54Z must be refused, the quoted form accepted (without which the negative control is satisfied by a gate that refuses everything), and a third fixture asserting three distinct verdicts. That is mutation-adjacent testing done by hand, and it exists because a script had claimed # Pinned by litmus:... for weeks against a test nobody had written — now itself gated by scripts/check-litmus-pin-claims.sh. scripts/litmus-stdlib.sh supplies mf_stage/mf_holds* because a producer | grep -q pipeline yields one status for two stages and SIGPIPEs its producer; enabling set -o pipefail was measured to move 4 of 249 tests to FAIL, all four false positives, zero masked producer failures — and the fix chosen was the consumer reading a complete buffer, not the flag. Also measured: mf_holds shipped as ERE while 321 of 460 corpus sites used BRE grep -q, and the pattern fail:...:state={state} errors under ERE. One variant per grep flag. Correct.

Where the claims outrun the evidence

Now the part you came for.

  • crates/tillandsias-litmus does not exist as code. It has a README.md and src/mock/podman.rs.example. It is not a workspace member (Cargo.toml lists only tillandsias-litmus-rust). Yet methodology/litmus-framework.yaml specifies four layers with named files — src/signal/registry.rs, src/test/graph.rs, src/convergence/centicolon.rs — and methodology/litmus-centicolon-wiring.yaml instructs agents that "the complex arithmetic ... is managed by the tillandsias-litmus CLI" and that they "MUST NOT attempt to calculate CentiColon budgets and residuals manually." The authority delegated to is a .rs.example.
  • The CentiColon obligation model is unimplemented. proximity.yaml specifies weights (must_requirement: 100, invariant: 120, negative_litmus_signal: 100), multipliers, six cap_rules, and sixteen penalties (ghost_trace: -50 … metric_gaming_suspected: -120). Grep the tree: not one of those identifiers appears outside methodology/ and the .example. What actually computes the score is scripts/local-ci.sh's check_weight() — a hardcoded table over 13 CI checks whose pre-build subset sums to exactly the total_cc: 990 in the committed dashboard. The "89.9% closed" is 890/990 over thirteen shell checks. It is a CI pass-rate wearing a lattice's clothes. No caps, no penalties, no per-spec rollup, no denominator-scope-change event.
  • The atomic unit of that model is missing from 92% of specs. Earning requires requirement_has_stable_id. Of 177 spec files, 15 carry a **ID**: field, against 2141 occurrences of MUST.
  • versioning.yaml documents a scheme the project retired. It defines v<Major>.<Minor>.<YYMMDD>.<Build> with Major = contract version and Minor = feature phase. The live format, per the 2026-08-31 operator ruling pinned in litmus-versioning-shape.yaml, is <years_since_epoch>.<month>.<day>.<build> — hence 56.9.2.1. The join-semilattice argument survives; the semantics documented for two of the four components are false. The repo caught this once — the release ledger records that "the versioning-shape litmus still pinned the retired scheme (the drift-protection had drifted)" — and fixed the litmus, not the doctrine.
  • Spec status vocabulary leaks. spec-system.yaml closes it to {draft, active, deprecated, obsolete}; forge-as-only-runtime/spec.md declares Status: current. One file, but the constraint reads all_spec_elements_must_be_machine_parsable, and this element is not in the machine's alphabet. Adjacent: proximity.yaml line 397 contains the list item remove_or perturb_runtime_trace_emission_and_measure_trace_gap — a space where an underscore belongs, in a controlled vocabulary nothing validates.
  • The complexity constraint is breached by its own metric. convergence.yaml:methodology_complexity_constraint requires methodology_complexity / codebase_complexity < 0.15 and flags as a red flag "CI validators exceed 5000 lines". Methodology YAML is ~9,991 lines against ~192,634 lines of Rust — 0.052, comfortably inside — but that denominator quietly omits the 34,606 lines of litmus YAML and the ~16,225 lines of scripts/check-*.sh validators alone. The validators are past the stated red flag by more than 3×. The constraint is real; the measurement is not instrumented, so it has never fired.

What survives all of that is the interesting result: scripts/trace-coverage.sh reports specs=202 traced=184 ghost=18 annotations=4963 files=1585 live, and the ghost gate is a ratchet against openspec/ghost-trace-baseline.txt failing in both directions — new ghosts fail, and a baseline gone stale fails too, forcing the prune. The script's own header explains why it deleted 171 generated TRACES.md files: they looked like they discharged the required trace_coverage_summary evidence field and did not — a spec→file:line index is not a coverage summary — so a declared obligation sat open for its whole life behind ~4000 lines of per-cycle churn. Removing the rendering and computing the summary on demand closed the obligation in the same change.

That is the actual methodology, and it is better than its own scoring system: an event-intake file (methodology/event/, 33 numbered records) for observations the model did not predict, a bounded-uncertainty exception so monotonicity cannot force retention of false certainty, and a documented willingness to delete the artefact that was pretending to be evidence. The formal apparatus is sound and modest. The instrumentation that was supposed to make it binding is, at this revision, a thirteen-row weight table and a dashboard three weeks stale. Judge it on the former if you are assessing the thinking, and on the latter if you are assessing the claim.
